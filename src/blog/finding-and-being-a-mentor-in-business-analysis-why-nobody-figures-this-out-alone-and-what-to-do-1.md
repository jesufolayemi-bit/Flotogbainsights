The single biggest reason mentorship disappoints people is a mismatched expectation, set long before the first real conversation ever happens. Most people carry a quiet, half-formed hope that a mentor will hand them something close to a shortcut. A referral into a role. A problem solved on their behalf. A map with no ambiguity left anywhere on it. When the actual conversation turns out to be more modest than that, some people quietly conclude mentorship does not work for them, when what actually happened is that the expectation was wrong from the start.

## What a good mentor actually does

There are four things, consistently, across every good mentoring relationship I have been part of, on either side of the table.

1. They ask the question you did not think to ask yourself. Often the most valuable thing in a mentoring conversation is not the answer given; it is the sharper question that reframes a problem you had been thinking about the wrong way entirely.
2. They tell you which worries are real and which are noise. Early career anxiety rarely comes with a label attached explaining whether it is a genuine risk or a normal, survivable discomfort. A mentor who has already lived through the same stretch can usually sort that in a sentence, where it might take you months of quiet worry to sort out alone.
3. They shorten your feedback loop by reacting to your actual work, not your description of it. A mentor who reads your actual requirements document, or watches you run an actual stakeholder conversation, gives feedback grounded in reality rather than in whatever version of events you managed to describe second-hand.
4. They normalise the parts of the struggle you assumed were unique to you. Hearing directly from someone credible that a particular kind of stakeholder conflict, or a particular kind of self-doubt, is common rather than a sign something is wrong with you specifically, does real work that no amount of solo reflection can do on its own.

## What a good mentor does not do

It is just as important to be honest about the limits, because expecting a mentor to do any of the following is what turns a genuinely useful relationship into a source of quiet disappointment.

1. They do not do your work for you. A mentor can react to a draft requirements document, not write it. The actual craft still has to be built by you, through the doing, the same argument made in the Breaking Into Business Analysis series.
2. They do not guarantee an outcome. A mentor sharpening your interview answers does not guarantee you the job. They improve your odds by removing avoidable mistakes, which is different from removing all uncertainty.
3. They are not a substitute for your own judgment. The mentors worth having will tell you what they think and then expect you to weigh it against your own read of the situation, not simply defer to them by default.

## A conversation that shows the difference

| **WHAT A MENTOR'S HELP ACTUALLY SOUNDS LIKEYou:***I think I should push back on this deadline, but I'm worried it makes me look difficult this early in the project.***Mentor:***What's the actual risk if you don't push back?***You:***We'd be shipping with two known defects still open.***Mentor:***Then that's not a difficult conversation; that's a responsible one. The framing that makes it land well is naming the specific risk, not the general feeling that the date is tight. I'd flag it today rather than closer to the date.* |
| --- |

Notice the mentor did not draft the message, attend the meeting, or make the decision. They sharpened the thinking behind a decision the analyst was always going to have to make and act on themselves. That is the actual shape of good mentorship, in almost every case worth having.

## Why naming this matters before you go looking for one

Going into a search for a mentor with the right expectation changes how you evaluate the people you meet. Someone who asks you sharp questions and reacts honestly to your actual work is a better find than someone who makes big promises and offers vague encouragement. Tomorrow's article is a practical framework for finding and approaching exactly that kind of person, since knowing what you are actually looking for is most of the battle.

## The version of this I try to be

When someone I mentor brings me a genuinely hard situation, the instinct to just tell them what to do is strong, and I have learned to resist it more than I expected to need to. The moments that actually help someone are rarely the ones where I hand over an answer. They are the ones where a single question makes them realise they already knew what to do and had simply talked themselves out of trusting it. Holding back the urge to solve it for them, and asking the sharper question instead, is a harder discipline than it sounds, and it is the entire difference between mentoring someone and quietly doing their thinking for them.

It is worth adding one honest caveat before moving on. Not every mentoring conversation will feel useful in the moment, even with the right person and the right expectations. Some of the most valuable input I ever received felt underwhelming when I first heard it, and only proved its worth weeks later when the situation it applied to actually arrived. Judging a mentor, or the entire idea of mentorship, on a single conversation that did not land is the same mistake as judging a whole certification path on one difficult exam question. Give it more than one data point before deciding whether it is working.
