<strong>You cannot make yourself smarter on demand. But you can run a decision through a shape that catches the things your confidence is hiding, and that is most of what good judgement actually is.</strong>

This week has been about seeing clearly: catching your own bias, checking the frame, testing the evidence. Today is the practical kit, a small set of reasoning moves you can apply deliberately when a decision matters enough to deserve slow thinking. None of these is clever. Their power is that they force your mind down a path it would not take on its own, and in doing so, they surface the risk, the assumption, or the consequence you were about to walk straight past. You do not need all of them every day. You need to know they exist, so you can reach for the right one when the stakes are real.

## First principles: reason from what is actually true

Most of our thinking runs by analogy: this is how it has always been done, this is like that other thing, so we do what we did before. That is fast and usually fine, and occasionally it carries a bad assumption forward for years because nobody questioned it. First-principles thinking, an idea reaching back to Aristotle, means stripping a problem back to the few things you are genuinely sure are true and rebuilding from there. A team once knew a monthly report had to be produced because it always had been. Reasoning from first principles meant asking a plainer question: who reads it, and what decision do they make with it? The answers were nobody and none. The task that had eaten days for years simply stopped. When you ask what we actually know to be true here, rather than what we have always assumed, whole problems sometimes dissolve. The discipline is demanding precisely because it refuses the comfort of the familiar answer and makes you rebuild from the ground up, but it is the surest way to notice when an entire effort is standing on an assumption that was never true.

## Inversion and the pre-mortem: work back from failure

The mathematician Carl Jacobi advised his students to invert, always invert, and Charlie Munger made it famous in decision-making: many hard problems are best solved backwards. Instead of asking how do we succeed, ask how do we guarantee failure, and then avoid those things. The most practical form is the pre-mortem, developed by the psychologist Gary Klein. Before you commit to a plan, gather the people involved and imagine it is a year from now, and the plan has failed completely. Then ask everyone to write down why. Because you have given people permission to see failure, they say the doubts they would have swallowed in an optimistic planning session, and the risks you were quietly discounting, arrive in plain sight while there is still time to handle them.

1. State the plan, then say: it is a year from now, and this failed badly. Everyone, write down why, privately, for five minutes.
2. Read the reasons out. The honest ones, the political ones, the technical ones. No defending, just collecting.
3. Take the two or three most likely and most damaging, and build a response to each into the plan before you commit.

<strong>Second-order thinking: and then what?</strong>

Most poor decisions are not wrong in their first effect. They are wrong about what the first effect causes next. The investor Howard Marks calls the fix second-order thinking, and it is captured in a single question you ask of any decision: and then what happens. You add a discount to win the deal, and then what? Competitors match it, and then what? The whole market resets lower, and the discount you meant as a one-off becomes the new normal you cannot escape. First-order thinking stops at the immediate, pleasing result. Second-order thinking follows the chain two or three steps further, to the consequences of the consequences, which is where the real cost or benefit usually lives. Asking and then what a few times in a row is one of the cheapest ways to avoid a decision that looks smart today and expensive next quarter.

## The outside view: trust base rates over the story

When we judge our own project, we tell ourselves a story from the inside: our team, our plan, our reasons why this time will be different. Kahneman called the antidote the outside view. Instead of the inside story, you ask how similar efforts have generally turned out. How long do projects like this usually take? How often do rollouts like this slip? What is the base rate? The inside view says our migration will take three months because our plan says so. The outside view says migrations like this take nine months more often than not, so what do we know that the dozens of teams before us did not? Almost always, the honest answer is nothing, and the base rate is trying to tell you something your optimism is refusing to hear.

## Occam's razor: prefer the simpler explanation

When several explanations could account for what you are seeing, the one that needs the fewest new assumptions is usually the one to check first. This old rule, named for the medieval friar William of Ockham, is not a law of nature, and the simplest explanation is not always right. But it is the best place to start, because complicated theories have more ways to be wrong and demand more things to be true at once. When a report shows a strange number, the elaborate story about a systemic data failure is possible, but the simpler explanation, that someone changed a filter, is both more likely and far cheaper to rule out. Analysts waste enormous effort chasing sophisticated explanations for problems that turn out to have dull, simple causes. Check the dull, simple cause first. If it holds, you have saved a week. If it does not, you have lost ten minutes and earned the right to consider the complicated story.

Notice that these tools are not rivals. On a real decision, you reach for several: first principles to question the inherited framing, a pre-mortem to surface the risks, second-order thinking to trace the consequences, the outside view to check your optimism against reality, and the razor to stop you overcomplicating what is simple. Each is a different angle of light on the same decision, and each catches something the others miss. You are not choosing one tool. You are building the habit of running an important call past two or three of them before you commit, which takes minutes and routinely changes the answer.

## The mistakes that blunt these tools

The tools only help if you use them honestly, and a few habits quietly defeat them.

1. Running a pre-mortem as theatre, then ignoring what it surfaces because the plan is already emotionally set.
2. Stopping second-order thinking at the pleasant consequence and not following it to the unpleasant one.
3. Dismissing the base rate by saying "this time is different", which is true far less often than it feels.
4. Reaching for a tool to justify a decision you have already made, rather than to test one you have not.
5. Preferring the clever, complicated explanation because it feels more impressive than the boring one that is probably true.

## One thing to try this week

Pick your next real decision of any size, and before you make it, run a five-minute pre-mortem on yourself. Imagine it is six months from now, and the decision went badly. Do not ask whether it might fail. Assume it did, and ask why. The reasons your mind produces under that instruction are the risks you were quietly discounting, and they come far more honestly than they ever do when you ask the gentle version. Write them down, and handle the two most likely before you commit. That single habit, imagining the failure in order to prevent it, will do more for how often you are right than any amount of extra analysis, because it uses your imagination against your own optimism.
