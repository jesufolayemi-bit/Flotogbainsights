<strong>The decision meeting is usually where a decision is announced, not where it is made.</strong> It took me years and several painful losses to understand this. I used to pour everything into the meeting itself. I would build the perfect case, rehearse the presentation, anticipate the questions, and walk in ready. And often enough, the room would go a different way, and I would leave baffled, certain I had made the stronger argument. I had. It just did not matter, because the people in that room had quietly made up their minds days earlier, in conversations I was not part of, and the meeting was only ratifying decisions that were already mostly made.

Once you see this, your whole approach changes. You stop treating the meeting as an event and start treating it as the closing ceremony. The actual work of moving a decision happens before, in the small conversations where people form their views while they still feel open to forming them. If you only show up for the formal moment, you are arguing with minds that are already set.

## Start from what they care about, not what you want

When you have no authority, you cannot move a decision by wanting it more. You move it by connecting it to what the decision-maker already wants. So the first work is not building your argument. It is understanding the other person well enough to know what is actually driving them: what they are measured on, what they are afraid of, what would make them look good to the people they answer to, and what would make them look careless. Almost every decision-maker is solving for pressures you cannot see from your seat. I once spent three weeks unable to get a sensible data fix approved, quietly convinced the head of operations was just being stubborn. When I finally sat down with her properly, the whole picture changed. She was not against the fix at all. She had been burned the year before by a change that was rushed through and broke month-end, and her real fear was another nasty surprise that made her team look careless in front of finance. The moment I reframed the proposal around how we would protect month-end and keep her people clear of any blame, the objection simply dissolved. The fix had not changed by a single line. The framing had, because it finally spoke to the thing she was actually carrying.

## A framework: map the room before you enter it

Before any decision that matters, the people who reliably win them do a quick piece of mapping. The simplest version is a grid borrowed from stakeholder analysis, often called the power and interest grid. You place each person on two axes: how much power they have over this decision, and how much they care about it. The four corners then tell you exactly where to spend your limited time.

- <strong>High power, high interest</strong>: the people you must win in person, one to one, before the meeting. These are the decisions. Everything else is secondary.
- <strong>High power, low interest</strong>: keep them satisfied and, above all, unsurprised. Their indifference turns into opposition fast if they feel blindsided in public.
- <strong>Low power, high interest</strong>: your potential allies and your best source of ground truth. They often know the real objections before the powerful people voice them.
- <strong>Low power, low interest</strong>: keep them informed and move on. Spending your energy here is the most common way good time gets wasted.

The point of the grid is not the diagram. It is that it stops you from pouring your effort into the loudest person in the room instead of the person who actually decides, which is one of the most common ways a winnable decision gets lost.

## The pre-meeting ritual

Here is the practice that separates people who reliably move decisions from people who are reliably surprised. They never let an important decision happen for the first time in the formal meeting. They have already had the conversation, one-on-one, with each person who matters, before everyone is in the room together. This is not as cynical as it can sound. It is mostly respect and good sense. You go to the key people in advance and you say, in effect, here is what I am thinking, here is why, what am I missing, and what would worry you about this. You are not ambushing them or twisting arms. You are giving them the chance to think it through privately, raise concerns without an audience, and shape the idea before it hardens. People defend ideas they helped form.

## Why the private conversation works better

1. People are honest one-on-one in a way they never are in a group. The real objection, the one they would never voice in front of their peers, comes out when it is just the two of you.
2. You can address each person's specific worry on its own terms, instead of trying to satisfy a whole room with one argument that fits none of them perfectly.
3. Nobody likes being surprised in public. Walking someone through your thinking beforehand means they are never put on the spot, and people reward you for not making them look unprepared.

## How to run the pre-meeting groundwork

Put the grid and the private conversation together, and you have a simple sequence you can run before any decision that matters.

- Map first. Use the grid to find the few people whose yeses actually decide this, and ignore the noise from everyone else for now.
- Go to each of them privately and lead with their interest, not your proposal. Ask what would worry them about this direction before you sell anything.
- Listen for the real objection, the one they would never say in a group, then solve it or soften it before the room ever convenes.
- Recruit at least one respected ally who will speak for the idea in the meeting, so the room hears it from someone other than you.
- Walk in already knowing how each key person will react, because you have heard them all. No surprises, for them or for you.

## Make it easy to say yes

Even with the groundwork done, you can lose by making the yes feel costly. Lower the cost of agreement as far as it will go. That means doing the hard work yourself and bringing a clear recommendation rather than an open question, because most decision-makers do not want a pile of options and the burden of choosing. They want a competent person to say: here is what I would do and why, here is the main risk, and here is how we would handle it if it goes wrong. It also means leaving people a graceful exit. Never frame a decision so that changing course means admitting they were foolish before. "Given what we have learned since then, it might be worth revisiting" lets someone shift without losing face, where "you were wrong about this" guarantees they dig in. And when a decision genuinely goes against you after a fair hearing, the most influential thing you can do is disagree and commit. Say your piece honestly, and once the call is made, get behind it fully. The person who argues hard and then supports the outcome gracefully earns the right to be in the room for the next decision. The person who sulks or says "I told you so" does not.

## The mistakes that lead to losing winnable decisions

Most lost decisions are lost in a handful of predictable ways, and almost all of them happen before or during the meeting rather than on the merits of the case.

- Ambushing people with a big decision they are hearing for the first time in public. Surprise breeds resistance, even when the idea is good.
- Handing over options instead of a recommendation. Decision-makers want a competent view, not homework.
- Arguing your case in the room when you should have argued it in the corridor a week earlier.
- Reaching for someone senior to force the outcome, which wins this one decision and costs you the relationship for every decision after it.

## General everyday application

None of this is unique to business analysis. Anyone who has needed a group to decide something has either learned these moves or lost decisions they should have won. The parent building an agreement before a family decision, the volunteer moving a committee, the employee getting a new idea through a cautious organisation, they are all doing the same work: understanding what the other side cares about, doing the quiet groundwork in advance, and making the yes easy. A junior BA can take one idea from today, the pre-meeting conversation, and change the outcome of their next proposal. A senior one hopefully already does this on instinct and could not imagine walking into an important decision cold.
