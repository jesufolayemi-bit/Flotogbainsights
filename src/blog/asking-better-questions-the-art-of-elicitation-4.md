**Everything this week comes down to a single idea, and then a set of moves for acting on it.** The idea is that the words a stakeholder gives you are the start of the work, not the end of it. Their request is a clue, their tidy answer is a costume, and the real requirement is usually one or two honest questions deeper than where they stopped. Everything below is built to help you get to that deeper place quickly, under pressure, when a senior person is watching, and the easy thing is to just write down what you were told. Keep this one open beside you. The point of a playbook is not that it teaches you something new each time. It is that it holds the good habits steady on the days you are tired, rushed or out of your depth, which is most days. Print it, save it, pin it. Then use it.

## The four principles, in one breath each

1. People speak in solutions. Hear the request, then quietly chase the need behind it before you write anything down.
2. Order beats wording. Go wide before you go narrow. Discover with open questions, then confirm with closed ones, never the reverse.
3. The best knowledge is unspoken. Ask for the last time it went wrong, not the usual case, and watch the work where you can, instead of only hearing it described.
4. A room needs a referee. Quiet the loud, draw out the silent with private writing first, and never leave without reading the decisions back out loud.

## The question bank, sorted by what you need

These are the questions worth knowing by heart. They are grouped by the job they do, so you can reach for the right kind in the moment rather than hunting for words while a stakeholder waits.

## When someone hands you a solution

- "If this were already built, what would you do differently tomorrow morning?"
- "What are you doing today to work around not having it?"
- "What problem does this solve, and who feels that problem most?"

## When you are opening up a topic

- "Walk me through a normal week for your team."
- "Which part of that do you dread, and why?"
- "What happens if we change nothing at all?"

## When you want the hidden knowledge

- "Tell me about the last time this went wrong."
- "When does it not work the normal way?"
- "You said usually. When is it not usual?"
- "Why does that matter?" Then ask it again. And once more.

## When you need to confirm before you build

- "So if I have this right, the real blocker is X and not Y?"
- "What would have to be true for this to be wrong?"
- "Who else needs to agree before this is real?"

## The funnel, on a card

When you have five minutes to plan a conversation, plan the shape, not the script. The shape is always the same. Open the territory, find the friction, probe the friction with a story, then confirm and close. Wide to narrow. If your planning notes have closed questions at the top, you have built the funnel upside down, and you will confirm a set of answers nobody was given the chance to challenge.

## Running the room on a card

These are a few golden nuggets:

- Before you book it, finish two sentences: the one decision we must leave with is, and the people who can make it are.
- Invite the few who hold the knowledge or the decision. A focused five beats a comfortable twelve.
- Open hard questions with two minutes of private writing, then read the cards. The quiet expert's answer now lands with equal weight.
- Thank and redirect the dominant voice. Park the good tangent visibly. Name the dynamic when the room is stuck.
- Watch the energy, end on time, and read the decisions back before anyone leaves the room.

## Why this skill compounds

Here is the part for anyone wondering whether all this is worth the effort. Asking better questions is one of the few professional skills that genuinely compound across a whole career. Tools change. Frameworks come and go. Whole technologies arrive and reshape what we build. But the ability to sit with another human being and surface what they could not put into words themselves does not go out of date, and it gets quietly more valuable as everything around it gets automated.

This is also the skill that separates a BA that people tolerate from the one people request by name. Anyone can capture what was said, but the person who reliably uncovers what was meant, who walks out of the room with the real problem rather than the first solution, becomes the person stakeholders want in every important conversation. That reputation is not built on tools. It is built on questions, one good one at a time, over the years.

So whether you are a junior who printed this to pin to the wall, or a senior who recognised your own habits in it, the work is the same from here. Take one question from the bank into your next conversation. Ask it, then sit in the silence that follows long enough for the real answer to arrive. That silence, the one most people rush to fill, is where the whole craft lives. Thank you for spending the week here. Now go and ask a better question.

***Go out and be successful.***
