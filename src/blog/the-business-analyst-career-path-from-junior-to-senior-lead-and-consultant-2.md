**The move from senior to lead BA is the hardest transition on the whole path, because it asks you to give up the very thing that got you here: being the person who does the best work.** Up to this point, every stage rewarded you for what you personally produced. The junior BA is rewarded for reliable output, the senior BA for owning hard outcomes. Both are about your own hands. The lead is the first stage where that stops being true, and the change is so fundamental that many excellent analysts never make it, not because they lack the ability, but because they cannot let go. The lead's value is no longer what they produce. It is what they make possible in everyone around them. Your output becomes the team's output, and that is a genuinely different job from the one you were promoted out of. The trap has a familiar shape. The best analyst gets made lead, precisely because they were the best analyst, and then they keep doing the analysis, because that is what they are good at and what feels productive. They take the interesting problems for themselves, rewrite their team's work to their own standard, and stay the star player on a team that never learns to play. They are busy, they are producing, and they are failing at the actual job, because the actual job was never to do the work. It was to build a team that does.

## Multipliers and diminishers

Liz Wiseman studied this directly and drew a sharp line between two kinds of leaders. ***Multipliers*** make everyone around them smarter, more capable and more confident, so that the team's total output is far greater than the leader could ever produce alone. ***Diminishers***, often without meaning to, do the opposite: they hoard the thinking, take the interesting work, correct rather than develop, and slowly make everyone around them smaller and more dependent. The striking part of her finding is that many diminishers are brilliant, well-intentioned people who believe they are helping, when in fact their need to be the smartest person in the room is quietly capping everyone else.

This is the frame for the whole stage. ***Your job as a lead BA is to be a multiplier***, and almost everything that made you a great individual analyst pulls you toward being a diminisher instead. The instinct to jump in and fix, to take the hard problem because you will do it best, to correct the flawed document yourself rather than teach the person to see the flaw, all of it feels like leadership and all of it makes your team weaker. The discipline of the lead is to resist those instincts on purpose, and to measure yourself not by what you did this week but by what your people became able to do.

At the centre of this stage is a loss you have to accept. You have to give up being the one who does the best work, and take your satisfaction instead from watching someone else do it, sometimes not as well as you would have, and grow because you let them. This is emotionally harder than it sounds, because the work is where your identity and your pride have lived for your whole career so far. Handing a problem you would love to solve to someone who will solve it more slowly, and biting your tongue while they learn, goes against every instinct that made you good. But that handover is the job. A lead who cannot let go does not just limit themselves; they cap the entire team at the ceiling of their own personal capacity, which is the one thing a team was supposed to break through.

The pattern is common enough to be almost a rule. An organisation has a standout analyst, clearly the best on the team, and does the obvious thing: makes them the lead. For a while it looks like a triumph. The new lead is everywhere, reviewing everything, personally handling the hardest problems, rewriting the weaker work to the standard only they can hit. The output looks excellent, because it is largely theirs. Then the cracks show. Everything now has to pass through them, so everything waits on them. The talented people on the team, given only the scraps of work the lead did not keep, stop growing and start looking elsewhere. The lead is working eleven-hour days and cannot understand why the team feels slower than when they were an individual contributor. The tragedy is that it came from a strength, not a failing. They were too good at the work to stop doing it, and being the best individual doer was exactly the wrong instinct for the job they had been given. The team never became more than the one person at its centre, which is the precise opposite of what a lead is for. Every hour the lead spent doing the work was an hour not spent building people who could do it, and the bill came due slowly, in talent quietly walking out the door.

Three capabilities carry you through this stage.

1. The first is real delegation, which means handing over ownership of outcomes, not just tasks, and accepting the discomfort of someone else deciding how.
2. The second is coaching, developing people's judgement instead of rescuing their work, which usually means asking the question that helps them see the problem rather than simply telling them the answer.
3. The third is building standards and systems that outlast you: the templates, the ways of working, the shared bar for quality that raise the whole team's output whether or not you are in the room. The individual contributor solves the problem in front of them. The lead builds the thing that solves problems long after they have moved on.

## How to make the shift

You can build the lead's habits deliberately, and most of them run against your instincts.

1. Delegate the work you are best at, not just the work you dislike. Giving away your strengths is how the team grows and how you free yourself for the actual job.
2. Coach, do not rescue. When someone brings you a flawed piece of work, ask the question that helps them find the flaw, rather than fixing it yourself.
3. Build things that outlast you: a standard, a template, a way of working, so the team gets better even when you are not looking.
4. Measure your week by what your people produced and became able to do, not by what you personally shipped.

The failures here are subtle, because they look like hard work and high standards. So watch out for these behaviours:

1. Staying the best player, hoarding the interesting problems, and leaving the team to develop on the scraps.
2. Rewriting people's work to your own standard instead of teaching them to reach it, so they never actually grow.
3. Micromanaging the how, which tells capable people you do not trust them and slowly drives the good ones away.
4. Measuring your own worth by your personal output, and so quietly resenting the job you were promoted into.

The lead who will not let go pays a price that is easy to miss, because from the outside they look impressive: always busy, always producing, always the one who saved the day. But underneath, their whole team is capped at the limit of what that one person can personally touch, and the talented people on it, the ones who needed room to grow, quietly leave for somewhere they are trusted with real ownership. The lead becomes the bottleneck they were promoted to remove, and the cruel part is that it comes from their strength, not their weakness, from being too good at the work to stop doing it. The result is a team that never becomes more than its leader, when the entire point of the role was to build one that does. Choosing to multiply instead of to do is not a soft skill at this stage. It is the whole job.

***Go out and be successful.***
