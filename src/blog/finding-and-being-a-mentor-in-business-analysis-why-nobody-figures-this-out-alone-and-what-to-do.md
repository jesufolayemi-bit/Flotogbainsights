I studied computer engineering, did a masters in software technology, and somewhere in the middle of it had to admit something that felt almost embarrassing at the time. I did not have a flair for coding. Everyone pointed me toward the obvious consolation route for someone who could not code. IT support. A stack of certifications. I refused a career chosen by default and went looking for a different one instead, a role that used the way I actually think and sat close to the technology I loved. I found a school that trained people in business analysis properly, and I started from zero, in a field where I had no network, no track record, and no obvious next step once the course ended.

What actually closed that distance was not the course itself, as good as it was. It was one person, a few steps further along than me, who agreed to answer my questions honestly. They shortened years of trial and error into a handful of direct conversations, told me the things nobody puts in a textbook, and reminded me I belonged in the field exactly when I doubted it most.

## The Mentor Gap

Here is the pattern I now see clearly, both in my own career and in the analysts I mentor today. Most people try to solve every hard career stretch through more solo effort. More reading. Another certification. More hours spent quietly puzzling something out alone. That instinct is not laziness or a lack of drive, it is usually the opposite, a genuine belief that the responsible thing to do is figure it out yourself before asking anyone for help. The problem is that some knowledge simply does not live in a book or a course, because it only exists inside someone else's lived experience. How a specific kind of stakeholder actually behaves under pressure. What a hiring manager is really listening for in an interview answer. Which certification genuinely matters and which one is mostly for the wall, covered a few weeks ago in this series. None of that is secret information being deliberately withheld. It simply has never been written down anywhere, because it lives in the heads of people who have already lived through the specific situation you are facing for the first time.

## Why solo effort is the slow way, not the safe way

This is the reframe that matters most. Figuring everything out alone feels responsible, even admirable, but it is very often the slower and more expensive path, not the safer one. Every month spent solving something through trial and error that a five minute conversation could have shortened is a month of avoidable mistakes, avoidable rework, and avoidable self doubt, all paid for in the name of independence. A mentor does not make the work easier in the sense of doing it for you. What they actually do is remove the tax of learning everything the hard way, the tax every profession quietly charges anyone who insists on discovering its unwritten rules entirely on their own.

## What this actually looked like

<div class="callout">

<strong>A REAL KIND OF CONVERSATION A MENTOR MAKES POSSIBLE</strong>

<strong>You:</strong>

<em>I keep getting asked in interviews to describe a time I handled conflicting stakeholder priorities, and I never know how much detail is too much or too little.</em>

<strong>Mentor:</strong>

<em>Keep it to one specific situation, not a general philosophy. State what the two priorities actually were, what you did, and what happened after. Thirty seconds, not three minutes. Interviewers are listening for whether you can be concise under pressure just as much as they're listening for the answer itself.</em>

</div>

That exchange took under a minute. Working out that lesson alone, through a string of interviews that went slightly wrong for reasons that were never quite clear, could easily have taken months, and the cost would not have been abstract, it would have been actual missed opportunities along the way.

None of this argues that mentorship replaces effort, certifications, or real project experience. A mentor cannot do the requirements gathering for you, cannot sit the exam on your behalf, and cannot build the evidence covered in the Building Your Brand series in your place. What a mentor changes is the direction and speed of the effort you were always going to have to put in anyway, by removing the guesswork about what actually matters and what is a waste of time.

## Why I keep coming back to this specific gap

I have mentored a good number of people into this field now, and the pattern repeats with a consistency that still catches me off guard. The analysts who come to me stuck are rarely stuck on something genuinely difficult. They are stuck on something that would take five minutes to clear up with the right person, and they have been carrying it alone for months, sometimes years, because asking never occurred to them as a realistic option.

This is not a criticism. It is exactly the position I was in myself, in a field with no network, no obvious next step, and no idea whether the mistakes I was making were normal or catastrophic. The only thing that changed that was someone willing to tell me plainly which was which. That is a small thing to give, and it is nearly impossible to give it to yourself, no matter how capable or self aware you are.
