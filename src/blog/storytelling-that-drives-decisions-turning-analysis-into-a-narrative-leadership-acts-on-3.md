**The same story has to be re-cut for the person who has to act on it, because a decision-maker is not a smaller version of your team. They are a different audience with a different need.**

You can have a true finding, the right narrative shape, and clear, concrete data, and still lose the decision if you deliver it as though the executive has the time and context of someone who sat beside you through the work. They do not. They have minutes, competing priorities, and a healthy scepticism built from years of people bringing them half-baked cases. Today is about cutting the story to fit that reality, so that the person who can actually say yes has everything they need to say it, and nothing that gets in the way. This is where all the earlier pieces come together and get aimed at a single target. The structure from Tuesday and the data discipline from Wednesday were about making the case clear. Now the case has to be made clear to a specific, busy, powerful person, and that means leading differently, choosing what to keep, and being explicit about what you want in a way that can feel uncomfortable until you see how much better it works.

## Lead with the bottom line

With an executive audience, the answer-first rule stops being a preference and becomes non-negotiable. Open with your conclusion and your ask in the first minute, before any build-up. This feels wrong to most analytical people, because we are trained to earn the conclusion by walking through the evidence first. But a decision-maker who does not know where you are heading cannot judge whether to keep listening, and will spend your whole build-up quietly anxious about where this is going. Tell them the destination immediately: here is what I found, here is what I recommend, here is what I need from you. Then spend the rest of the time supporting it, going only as deep as their questions require. You are not withholding the reveal for a dramatic finish. A decision-maker does not want a mystery. They want the answer, and then the confidence to trust it.

The Heath brothers, in their work on why some ideas stick, found that memorable ideas tend to be simple, concrete, and built around something the mind can grasp and carry. For a decision-maker, this means giving them one sticky thing, usually a single number or a single line, that captures the whole case, and that they can repeat to their own boss without you there. Because that is what actually happens: your executive does not forward your deck up the chain, they repeat one sentence in a corridor, and if you have not chosen that sentence for them, they will invent a worse one or forget entirely.

So decide, in advance, what the one line is. We are losing a support team's annual budget every year to a broken mobile checkout, and I can fix it before the next campaign. That is portable. It survives being repeated. Everything else in your presentation exists to earn and support that one line, and the discipline of choosing it forces you to work out what your case actually is, stripped to its core. If you cannot compress it to one repeatable sentence, you do not yet know what you are asking for.

## Tailor the story to what they care about

The same finding matters to different leaders for different reasons, and the story should bend to the person in the room. A finance-minded executive hears cost and risk. A growth-minded one hears opportunity and speed. A leader who was burned last year hears whatever protects them from being burned again. This is not manipulation; it is translation: connecting your true finding to the thing this particular person is actually responsible for and worried about. Before the meeting, ask yourself what this specific person is measured on and afraid of, and lead with the version of your case that speaks to it. The same broken checkout is a revenue-recovery story to one leader and a customer-experience risk to another, and both are true.

## Make the ask impossible to miss

A surprising number of presentations end with no clear ask at all. The analyst presents, the room says thank you, and nothing happens, because nobody was ever told what to do. An executive audience should never have to guess what you want. State it plainly: the decision you need made, the resource you need approved, the direction you need endorsed, and by when. Make it a single, specific, answerable request, not a vague hope that they will be inspired to act. The clearer the ask, the easier the yes, and the more often good analysis turns into actual movement. So do the following

1. **Name the decision**: what exactly are you asking them to approve, choose, or endorse?
2. **Name the stakes**: what happens if they say yes, and what happens if nothing is decided?
3. **Name the timing**: by when does this need a decision, and why that date?
4. **Keep the depth in reserve**: hold the detailed evidence in an appendix, ready for the question, out of the main line.

## Hold your ground in the questions

The presentation is only half the encounter. The other half is the questions, and this is where a decision-maker quietly decides whether to trust you. The instinct under pressure is to answer every question in full depth, which drags you straight back into the data dump you worked so hard to avoid. Resist it. Answer the question directly and briefly first, then stop, and go deeper only if they want more. This is exactly why you keep the detailed evidence in reserve: a question is an invitation to reach for the right piece of it, not a cue to empty the whole appendix onto the table.

Two things protect you here. ***Knowing your material cold***, so you can go as deep as any question requires without losing the thread. And ***being honest about the edges of what you know***, because the fastest way to lose a decision-maker is to bluff an answer and be caught. "I do not know, I will confirm and come back to you by Thursday" does more for your credibility than a confident guess that later turns out wrong. The goal in the questions is not to win every exchange. It is to leave the room more confident in you than when you walked in.

The practical test of all this is compression. Take your full analysis and cut it to a three-minute brief, on purpose, as an exercise.

1. Start with the one repeatable line.
2. Add the tension: the but, stated as their problem.
3. Add the single most convincing number, made concrete.
4. Add the ask, made explicit.

That is the whole thing, and if it holds together at three minutes, it will hold at thirty, with room to spare for questions. If it falls apart when compressed, the long version was never a case; it was a pile of material wearing the costume of one.

## The mistakes that lose the room

A few habits reliably cost you a decision you had already earned with the analysis.

1. Saving the point for the end, so it arrives after the audience has stopped listening.
2. No clear ask, so a willing room has nothing specific to say yes to.
3. Drowning the case in detail to prove rigour, and losing the one thing you needed them to remember.
4. A generic pitch that ignores what this particular decision-maker actually cares about.

## One thing to try this week

Before your next presentation of any size, write the one sentence you want the most senior person in the room to repeat to someone else afterwards. Just that sentence, before you build a single slide. Make it true, make it concrete, make it carry the stakes and point at the action. If you cannot write it, you are not ready to present, because you do not yet know what your case is. If you can, you now have the spine of the whole thing, and every slide becomes either support for that line or something to cut. That one habit, deciding the sentence first, will do more for whether your work gets acted on than any amount of polish on the deck.

***Go out and be successful.***
