**Influence without authority comes down to three things: get heard, build trust, and move the decision. Here is the week on one page.** A playbook is not there to teach you something new each time you open it. It is there to hold the good habits steady on the days you are rushed, outranked, or talking to a room that has every right to ignore you. So before the worked example, here is the whole week, distilled to what you would actually carry into a conversation.

## The week in four short passes

- **Monday was about the ground you stand on**. Authority is borrowed and runs out the day you hand back the title. Influence is earned and compounds for the rest of your career. French and Raven's old map of power still holds: the positional kinds, legitimate, reward and coercive, belong to the chair, while the personal kinds, expert and referent, belong to you and are available with no title at all. The shift that matters is to stop trying to be obeyed and start trying to be believed.
- **Tuesday was about getting heard**, because the best idea is worthless if it never lands. The curse of knowledge makes experts start halfway across the river, so lead with the point instead, the way the Minto Pyramid and the military's bottom-line-up-front both insist. Say it in the listener's language, connect it to what they care about, and keep it short, because brevity is a form of respect for their attention.
- **Wednesday was about trust**, the ground on which everything else stands. The trust equation is a clean way to work on it: credibility plus reliability plus safety, all divided by how self-interested you appear. Build the top through small promises kept and honest admissions of what you do not know. Protect the bottom by caring more about the right answer than the credit. Remember the emotional bank account: deposits are small, withdrawals are large, and that asymmetry is exactly what makes trust worth having.
- **Thursday was about moving the decision**, which is mostly won before the meeting. Map the room with the power and interest grid so you spend your time on the people who actually decide. Have the quiet one-to-one conversations first, surface the real objection in private, and make the yes easy by bringing a recommendation rather than a pile of options. When the call goes against you after a fair hearing, disagree and commit, and earn your seat at the next decision.

## Watch all four work together

In real life, these never arrive one at a time, so here is a single situation that needs all of them. Say you want to retire an old report that three teams still rely on every month, because the numbers in it have quietly become unreliable. You cannot order anyone to stop using it. You have no authority here at all, which is exactly the point. Plenty of people would either send a blunt email announcing the report is being switched off and meet a wall of resistance, or give up and let the bad numbers keep circulating. The path between those two failures is the whole week put together.

**Influence comes first.** With no power to compel anyone, you lean on the personal kind you have built: people know your judgement on data is sound, and they know you are not chasing a win.

**Then getting heard.** You do not open with a lecture on data lineage. You send each team lead a short note that leads with the point: this report is now giving you numbers you cannot trust, and here is what I suggest we do about it.

**Then trust does its quiet work**. Because you have spent months keeping small promises with these teams, they believe you when you tell them the figures are off, rather than assuming you are empire-building.

**Finally, you move the decision**. You map who actually owns the call, and you discover one team uses the report for the board pack, which makes its owner the high-power, high-interest person you must win first. You have the private conversation, and the real fear surfaces: not the report itself, but looking unprepared in front of the board. So you solve that directly, bringing a clean replacement and a migration plan that protects the board pack, and you let everyone agree without anyone having to admit they relied on bad numbers for a year. The report gets retired. Not one part of that required authority. Each pillar did its job, and together they moved something that an order would only have forced halfway, leaving resentment behind it.

## Why this skill compounds

Influence is one of the very few professional skills that keep paying back across an entire career. Tools change, titles change, whole industries reshape themselves, but the ability to get heard, to be trusted, and to move people who do not have to move does not go out of date, and it grows more valuable as more mechanical work around it gets automated away. It is also the skill that decides how far your other skills travel. You can be the most capable person in the building, and if you cannot bring people with you, that capability stays trapped in your own head. So whether you saved this to return to or recognised your own habits in it, the work from here is the same. Take one move into your next conversation. Lead with your point. Keep one small promise you might have let slide. Have a quiet word before the meeting instead of saving it all for the room. Influence is built in these small choices, repeated, until one day people follow you for no reason other than that they have decided you are worth following. Thank you for spending the week here. Now go and move something.

***Go out and be successful.***
