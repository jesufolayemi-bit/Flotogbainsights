A hiring manager will forget the letters after your name within the hour of reading your resume. A genuinely good work sample sticks with them for weeks, sometimes long enough to be the reason they call you back over someone else with a stronger credential and nothing to show for it. This is not an argument against certifications. It is an argument for spending at least as much energy on the evidence a certification cannot produce, because for most analysts, that evidence is sitting unbuilt while the certification gets all the attention.

## The case study: your best single asset

A one page case study of a real problem you solved is the single highest return piece of evidence most analysts never build. Not a resume bullet point, an actual page: the situation, what you did, and what changed, written the way you would tell it to a colleague over coffee, not the way you would write it for an exam.

The structure that works consistently is short. Two or three sentences on the situation and why it mattered. A short paragraph on what you actually did, specific enough to be credible. A closing line on the outcome, with a real number where you have one and an honest, clearly stated result where you do not. Following the confidentiality discipline from the [<strong><em>Writing About Real Work Without Breaking Trust</em></strong>](/post/building-your-brand-as-a-ba-getting-known-for-the-work-you-already-do-well-3) article, generalise the industry and the exact figures enough that no one in the room could trace it back to a specific client, while keeping enough detail that the story still lands.

## The artefact: proof over description

A single strong artefact, a process map, a well structured requirements document, a stakeholder analysis, does something a case study cannot. It lets a hiring manager see your actual thinking laid out on a page, rather than taking your word for how you think. This is the single most underused piece of evidence in most BA job searches, mainly because people assume they cannot share anything from real client work.

You do not need to share the real artefact. Rebuild a disguised, generalised version of it from scratch, using the same structure and logic but a fictional scenario and fictional numbers, close enough to real work to prove the skill and far enough from any real client to raise no confidentiality concern at all. A single well built artefact like this, kept ready in your Featured section or a simple portfolio link, does more in a first interview than a paragraph describing your skills ever will.

## The reference: one sentence beats ten adjectives

A generic LinkedIn recommendation full of adjectives, thorough, professional, a pleasure to work with, gets skimmed and forgotten in seconds. A single reference who can say one concrete sentence about a specific thing you did carries far more weight, because specificity is what makes a recommendation sound earned rather than reflexive.

<div class="callout">

<strong>ASKING FOR THE RIGHT KIND OF REFERENCE</strong>

<strong>You:</strong>

<em>Would you be willing to write a short reference for me? If it's easier, I'm happy to draft something and you can edit it, but the one thing that would help most is one specific example, like the approvals process rework we did together, rather than a general note.</em>

<strong>Former colleague:</strong>

<em>Happy to. I'll mention the approvals rework, that cut our review time in half and I still point to it as an example of good analysis work.</em>

</div>

That single sentence, attached to a name and a real title, will outperform a paragraph of generic praise in almost every hiring conversation, because it gives the reader something they can actually picture rather than a list of adjectives they have read a hundred times before.

None of this argues against pursuing the right certification at the right time, covered earlier this week. It argues against treating a certification as a substitute for evidence, when in practice they answer two different questions for two different readers. A certification tells an automated filter and a skimming recruiter that you cleared a bar. A case study, an artefact, and a real reference tell an actual human, in the room, that you can do the job they are hiring for. Most careers need both, and most business analysts, focused entirely on the credential, have built neither.

## When to actually use each piece

The case study earns its place early, on your profile and in your Featured section, where it works quietly before any interview even happens, doing the job of getting you shortlisted in the first place. The artefact earns its place mid-process, sent ahead of a second interview or referenced directly when a panel asks how you actually approach the work, since it rewards a reader willing to look closely rather than skim. The reference earns its place last, closest to an offer, when a hiring manager is choosing between two strong finalists and one concrete sentence from someone who has actually managed you can be the deciding factor. Building all three does not mean deploying all three at once. It means having each one ready for the exact moment it will do the most work.
