<strong>A hiring manager told me once that she stopped shortlisting candidates based on certifications three years before we spoke.</strong> Not because she thought certifications were worthless, but because she had hired two CBAP holders back to back who could recite the BABOK guide almost verbatim and could not run a stakeholder workshop without a script sitting in front of them. The credential had proven they understood the theory. It had told her almost nothing about whether they could do the job in a room full of people who did not care about the theory at all.

## The Signal Gap

This is the honest starting point for this entire week. A certification is a signal, not a skill. It tells anyone reading your profile that you studied a defined body of knowledge and passed a test about it. That is genuinely useful information. It is not the same information as whether you can do the work, and treating the two as interchangeable is where most of the disappointment with certifications comes from, on both sides of the hiring table. The gap shows up most clearly in exactly the moment it matters most. An exam rewards knowing the correct textbook answer to a scenario question. A real stakeholder meeting rewards reading a room, adjusting your approach mid-conversation, and handling a version of the scenario that never quite matches the clean one in the study guide. Those are related skills, but they are not the same skill, and no exam has ever fully tested the second one.

None of this means that certifications are a waste of time, and it would be dishonest to pretend otherwise. They prove three things reliably, and all three have real value depending on your situation.

- You know the shared vocabulary of the profession. Terms like elicitation, traceability, and requirements baseline mean something specific, and a certification proves you speak that language rather than approximating it.
- You can commit to structured, sustained effort outside of work. Studying for CBAP while holding down a full-time job is a genuine test of discipline, and some hiring managers weigh that discipline heavily, independent of the content itself.
- You clear an automated filter. A meaningful share of larger companies and recruiters use certifications as a keyword filter before a human ever reads the resume. In that specific and unglamorous sense, a certification is not a signal of competence; it is a key that lets you into the room where competence can actually be evaluated.

However, it does not prove you can run a difficult stakeholder conversation. It does not prove you can write a requirements document a developer can actually build from without three rounds of clarifying questions. It does not prove judgment, which is the thing that actually separates a senior BA from a junior one, and judgment is built entirely through doing the work under real conditions, not through studying a framework for how the work is supposed to go.

Here is roughly how this gap surfaces in an actual interview, when a hiring manager is trying to work out which side of the certification a candidate is actually standing on.

<div class="callout">

<strong>TESTING THE SIGNAL AGAINST THE SUBSTANCE</strong>

<strong>Interviewer:</strong>

<em>I see you're CBAP certified. Walk me through a time a stakeholder flatly rejected a requirement you'd written and how you handled it.</em>

<strong>Candidate A:</strong>

<em>The BABOK guide recommends using elicitation techniques to clarify requirements before finalising documentation, and I always follow that structured approach.</em>

<strong>Interviewer:</strong>

<em>Sure, but can you walk me through an actual situation, what the stakeholder said and what you did next?</em>

<strong>Candidate A:</strong>

<em>I generally follow the standard requirements traceability process outlined in the certification.</em>

</div>

That exchange, or a close version of it, is exactly what trained the hiring manager I mentioned earlier to stop trusting the credential on its own. Compare it to a second candidate answering the same question.

<div class="callout">

<strong>THE VERSION THAT LANDS</strong>

<strong>Interviewer:</strong>

<em>Walk me through a time a stakeholder flatly rejected a requirement you'd written.</em>

<strong>Candidate B:</strong>

<em>A finance stakeholder rejected an approval workflow I'd documented because it added two steps to something her team did dozens of times a day. I hadn't accounted for volume. I went back, sat with two of her analysts for an hour, and rebuilt it around a fast path for routine cases with the extra checks only triggering above a certain threshold. She approved it the same week.</em>

</div>

Nothing in that second answer references a certification at all, and it is a far stronger answer, because it demonstrates the actual capability the certification was only ever meant to be a proxy for.

## Why the gap persists even for people who mean well

This is not a story about certifications being a scam or the people who pursue them being lazy. The gap persists for a much more ordinary reason: studying for an exam and doing the job reward genuinely different habits, and most study plans do not build the second one at all. An exam rewards recall under time pressure and the ability to select the textbook-correct answer from four options. A stakeholder meeting rewards improvisation, reading tone and body language, and being comfortable with an answer that is not fully worked out yet.

The analysts who close this gap well tend to do one thing differently while they study: they deliberately practise the material on a real or realistic scenario as they go, rather than only working through practice questions. Reading a chapter on elicitation techniques and then actually running one, even informally, in a real meeting that week, does more to close the Signal Gap than another hour of flashcards. The certification and the competence can grow together. They just do not grow together automatically.

So, the right question is not whether certifications matter. It is which certification, at which point in your career, actually closes a gap you genuinely have, rather than adding a credential to a profile that is already missing something more fundamental.
