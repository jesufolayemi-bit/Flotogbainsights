**I have never once been able to make anyone do anything.** I mean that literally. Across a long career as a business analyst, I have never had a person report to me on a project. I have never signed off on a budget or approved a hire. If I want a developer to build something a particular way, I cannot tell them to. If I want a director to back a decision, I cannot order it. By every measure the org chart cares about, I have no power. And the job, the actual job, is to change what an entire organisation chooses to build. For years, I treated this as the hard part. I thought the people with real titles had it easy, that they could simply decide and have it done, while I had to persuade my way through every conversation. It took me a long time to see that I had it backwards. Having no authority was not the obstacle. It was the thing that forced me to learn the skill that actually matters, the one that keeps working long after any particular title has been handed back.

## Responsibility without power is the normal condition now

If this sounds like a niche complaint about one job, look around. The modern workplace runs on people who are responsible for outcomes they cannot command. The specialist whose expertise everyone needs but who manages nobody. The project lead borrowing people from five different teams. The new joiner with a good idea and no standing to push it. The expert in a room full of people who outrank them. Almost everyone now has to get things done through others they do not control.

We have spent a century telling a story about leadership in which the leader stands at the top and issues direction down a clean line. That story was never very accurate, and it is barely recognisable now. Real work happens sideways, across teams, between people who have no formal hold on each other. Which means the most useful skill you can build is not climbing high enough to give orders. It is learning to move people who do not have to move.

## A map of what you actually have: the six bases of power

In 1959, two social psychologists, John French and Bertram Raven, mapped where power in organisations really comes from, and the map has held up for more than sixty years. They found that influence flows from six sources, and the distinction between them is the most useful thing you can understand about your own position. Three of the six are ***positional,* handed to you by the role you occupy.

- Legitimate power is the plain authority of the title.
- Reward power is your control over things people want, like money, promotion or recognition.
- Coercive power is your ability to punish.

These three come with the chair, and they vanish the moment you leave it. The other three are ***personal***, attached to you rather than to the chair.

- Expert power is the influence that comes from people trusting your knowledge and judgement.
- Referent power is the pull you have when people respect you, like working with you, and care about your opinion.
- Informational power is holding knowledge that others need.

Here is why this matters more than any pep talk about confidence. The positional kinds are exactly the ones most of us do not have. But the two that travel between jobs and grow over a whole career, ***expert and referent power***, are fully available to someone with no title at all. Every hour spent wishing for legitimate power is an hour not spent building the two forms that would actually serve you for the next twenty years.

**Authority is borrowed. Influence is earned.**

This is the distinction underneath the framework, and it changed how I work. Authority is something the organisation lends you. It comes with the role, it works because people fear the consequences of ignoring it, and it disappears the moment you leave the role. Authority is rented. It is real, but it is shallow, and people comply with it without believing in it.

Influence is different in kind, not just degree. It is built person by person, and it works because people actually believe you and want to go along with you. It does not vanish when your title changes because it was never attached to the title. Picture two people trying to get the same change made. The first sends a directive leaning on their seniority, and the team does the minimum to close the ticket, with no care and no warning when something downstream looks wrong. The second has spent months being useful, reliable and fair, so when they ask for the same change, the team not only does it well, but they flag the three problems the requester had not even thought of, because they want that person to succeed. Same change, same organisation, completely different result. The first got compliance. The second got commitment, and commitment is worth many times more, because it brings the other person's judgement along with their hands.

## Three traps that keep people stuck

When you have responsibility without authority, there are three tempting ways to cope, and all of them fail.

- The first is to wait. You tell yourself that once you are promoted, once you have the title, then you will get things done, so you sit on your good ideas waiting for permission that may never come. Even if it does come, you arrive at the senior role having never learned to move anyone, leaning on a borrowed power you do not know how to use.
- The second trap is to fake it, borrowing authority you do not have by invoking someone senior or pulling rank you were never given. It works once or twice, and the moment people notice, you have spent the only thing that was ever going to help you, which is their trust.
- The third trap feels like strength: going over someone's head the instant they resist. It wins you this one decision and quietly costs you the relationship for every decision after it, because the person you went around will remember, and next time they will not bring you their honest objection, they will just block you more carefully. None of these roads goes anywhere. The only one that does is the slow one.

## How to build influence from nothing

If positional power is off the table, you build the personal kind deliberately. There is a sequence to it that works whether you are new in a role or rebuilding standing after a setback.

1. Be useful before you need anything. Influence is banked in advance. Help people with their problems when you have no ask of your own, so the relationship already exists before the day you need it.
2. Be reliably right in your area. Expert power grows every time your judgement proves sound, and you protect it by never claiming more certainty than you actually have.
3. Make other people look good. The fastest route to referent power is helping others succeed and letting them keep the credit. People remember who made them look capable in front of their own boss.
4. Keep your asks small and your value large for as long as you can, so that by the time you do need something big, you are drawing on a long history of giving rather than asking on credit.

## The shift: stop trying to be obeyed, start trying to be believed

Everything turns on a single move. Stop asking how to make people do what you want, and start asking why anyone would choose to. The first question has no good answer when you hold no power. The second has answers you can work on every day: people go along with someone they trust, who makes their thinking clear, who understands what they care about, and who has been right and reliable before. None of that requires a title, and all of it can be built.

Having no authority is an advantage because it forces you to earn agreement honestly every time. The manager who can fall back on orders never has to learn this. You do, so you get genuinely good at it, far better than people who never had to, and that skill follows you everywhere. When someone agrees with you because they chose to, that agreement is real in a way compliance never is. They will defend it when you are not in the room. Orders do not get defended. Beliefs do.

## Where the week goes from here

Influence without authority rests on three things, and each gets its own day this week: getting heard, building trust, and moving the decision when you cannot cast the deciding vote. For today, just sit with the reframe. You may have less authority than you would like. Almost everyone does. But authority was never the thing that moved people anyway.

***Go out and be successful.***
