**The whole path comes down to one idea: at every stage, moving up means letting go of the thing that made you good at the stage before. Here is the map on one page.**

A career map is not there to tell you something new each time you look at it. It is there to help you see where you actually are, and what to build next, on the days you feel stuck without knowing why. So before the self-assessment, here is the whole week in one view, and the single engine that drives every step of it.

## The four stages, in one line each

- The junior BA executes: they do the defined work reliably, and the job is to become genuinely useful and dependable while building deep craft and real knowledge of the business.
- The senior BA owns: they take ambiguous problems with no clear brief and are trusted to figure out what needs doing, which is built on judgement, a point of view, and influence.
- The lead BA multiplies: their value becomes the team's output rather than their own, which demands delegation, coaching, and the hard letting-go of being the best individual doer.
- The consultant advises: they sell judgement and perspective across many organisations, which rests on range, reputation, and trust. Four stages, four different jobs.

The single most important thing to understand about this path is that it is not additive. Each stage is not the last one plus a bit more. Each transition requires giving something up. The junior BA must let go of needing to be told what to do. The senior BA must let go of being the one who personally does the best work. The lead BA must let go of the team as the limit of their reach and learn to think across organisations. Marshall Goldsmith built a whole book around this, and the phrase is worth keeping close: what got you here will not get you there. The behaviours that earned your last promotion are usually the exact behaviours you now have to abandon to earn the next one. Careers stall not when people stop working hard, but when they keep doing, faster and better, the thing that used to work.

## The plateaus where people get stuck

Each transition has a matching trap, and naming them helps you spot your own. There is the reliable junior BA who never develops a point of view, and stays an excellent executor waiting to be told. There is the senior BA who cannot let go, who gets made lead and quietly becomes the bottleneck, capping the team at their own capacity. There is the lead BA who never lifts their eyes beyond their own team to see the wider patterns, and so never grows into the outside perspective a consultant sells. The pattern is always the same shape: a strength from one stage, held on to too long, becomes the ceiling at the next. If you feel stuck, the question is rarely what more can I do. It is usually what am I refusing to let go of.

A simple way to locate yourself: look honestly at what people trust you with, and what you are still holding on to.

- If you do defined work well but wait to be told, you are building toward senior, and the thing to develop is a point of view on ambiguous problems.
- If you own hard outcomes but still measure yourself only by your own output, you are building toward lead, and the thing to practise is multiplying others.
- If you run a team well but have only ever worked in one context, you are building toward the wider perspective of a consultant, and the thing to seek is range and a visible reputation.

Notice that in every case, the next step is not more of what you are already good at. It is the beginning of the next stage, practised before you have the title, which is exactly what earns you the title. It is also worth being honest that not everyone wants to climb every rung, and that is a real choice, not a failure. A superb senior analyst who has no wish to lead a team is not stuck; they are exactly where they are useful and happy, and the point of the map is to help you choose your stage deliberately, not to insist that higher is always better.

Understanding the shape of the path is itself a career skill, and one that compounds, because it lets you build the next stage deliberately instead of stumbling into it years later. Most people discover each transition only after they have failed at it for a while: the senior who spent a year as a struggling lead before realising the job had changed, the expert who went independent and only then learned that reputation had to come first. Seeing the whole map lets you start building each stage before you arrive, so that when the opportunity comes you are already behaving like the person who belongs in it. That single habit, always asking what the next stage actually requires and beginning to practice it now, is worth more over a career than any tool or certification, because it never stops applying.

So wherever you are on this path, the work from here is the same. Find your stage honestly. Name the strength you are holding on to that has become your ceiling. And start practising the next stage before anyone gives you permission, because permission almost always follows the behaviour rather than leading it. The junior BA who thinks like an owner becomes a senior BA. The senior BA who multiplies becomes a lead BA. The lead who sees across contexts becomes an advisor. The title is just the recognition, arriving late, of a change you had already made. Thank you for spending the week here. Now go and build the stage you are growing into.

***Go out and be successful.***
