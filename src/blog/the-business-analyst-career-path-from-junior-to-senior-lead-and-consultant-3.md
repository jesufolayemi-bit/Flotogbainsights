<strong>The consultant is a different animal from everything that came before. You are no longer inside one organisation climbing a ladder. You are an outside expert, hired for judgement and perspective across many.</strong> This stage takes two broad forms, and the shift underneath them is the same. There is the internal principal or expert consultant, who has gone so deep that the organisation uses them to advise across teams rather than to work within one. And there is the independent consultant, who has left to build their own practice and sells their expertise to clients directly. In both cases, the fundamental change is what you are actually paid for. Up to now you were paid for your work, whether your own or your team's. As a consultant, you are paid for your judgement, and that is a stranger and harder thing to sell than it sounds. The reason it is harder is that judgement is invisible. Nobody can see it before they buy it. A junior BA's reliability shows in their delivery, a lead's value shows in their team, but a consultant's core asset lives in their head, and a prospective client has to trust it before they ever experience it. That single fact reshapes everything about this stage. It means your reputation is not a nice-to-have. It is the product, or at least the thing that makes the product sellable, and building it becomes as important as the expertise itself.

## What you actually sell

What a client is really buying, when they hire a good consultant, is pattern recognition and perspective. You have seen this problem before, not once but in a dozen organisations, and you know how it tends to go, where it usually breaks, and what actually worked, because you have watched it play out again and again. The people inside the client's organisation have only ever seen it in their own company, once, from the inside. You bring the outside view: the base rates, the patterns, the honest comparison to how others have handled the same thing, and the freedom to say the difficult thing precisely because you are not embedded in the politics. This is why range matters so much at this stage. The value of a consultant grows with the number of different contexts they have worked across, because each one adds to the library of patterns they can draw on. It is also why a consultant is worth more than the sum of their hours. You are not paying for the two days they spend with you. You are paying for the twenty years of pattern behind the judgement they deliver in those two days. The consultant who understands this sells the outcome and the insight, not the time, and prices accordingly. The one who still bills their expertise as an hourly rate has not fully made the transition in their own head.

## Trust is the real product

Because judgement is invisible, trust is what actually gets bought and sold at this stage. A client is handing important decisions to an outsider, which is a genuinely vulnerable act, and they will only do it with someone they believe is both capable and honestly on their side. The trusted advisor, in the sense David Maister wrote about, is not the smartest consultant in the market. It is the one clients believe will tell them the truth, including the truth they do not want to hear, and who cares more about the client's outcome than about extending the engagement. That reputation, once earned, is the most durable asset in a consulting career, because it brings the work to you rather than you chasing it.

An employee has months to prove themselves and build relationships. A consultant rarely has more than a meeting or two. You are often brought into a room full of people who did not choose you, may resent the implication that they needed outside help, and are watching to see whether you actually understand their world or are about to lecture them from a slide deck. So two abilities become central that employment never demanded at this intensity.

- The first is fast diagnosis: the knack of asking a few sharp questions and quickly forming an accurate picture of what is really going on, drawn from the patterns you have seen before.
- The second is earning trust quickly, which usually comes not from displaying how much you know, but from demonstrating that you understand their specific situation and are genuinely on their side.

The fastest way to lose a client's trust is to arrive with the answer before you have understood the question, the outside expert who pattern-matches too soon and prescribes the generic fix. The fastest way to earn it is the opposite: to show, in the first conversation, that you have grasped what makes their situation particular, and to be honest about what you do not yet know. Credibility at this stage is not the loudest expertise in the room. It is the accurate, well-aimed question that tells everyone present you have seen this before and are not going to waste their time.

Moving toward this stage means building a few things that employment never required of you.

1. You need depth plus range: real expertise in something specific, and enough breadth of context that you carry patterns others lack.
2. You need a visible reputation, because judgement that nobody knows about cannot be hired.
3. You need the ability to diagnose quickly and win trust fast, because you rarely have the luxury of time.
4. And you need the commercial side that employees never think about: positioning, a clear sense of who you help and how, pricing that reflects value rather than hours, and a pipeline so the work does not run dry.

So to get on this track:

- Build a niche and a reputation for it. Be known for something specific, so that the right problem brings your name to mind.
- Get range on purpose. Work across different organisations, industries and problems, because your patterns are your product and range is how you build them.
- Make your judgement visible. Write, speak and share how you think, so that people can trust your judgement before they have hired it. Reputation is built years before it is needed.
- Learn the business of the work: positioning, pricing on value, and keeping a pipeline, so the practice survives the quiet months.

## The mistakes that keep a consultant a contractor

There is a real difference between a consultant and a highly paid pair of outside hands, and a few habits keep people on the wrong side of it.

1. Selling hours and deliverables instead of outcomes and judgement, and pricing your years of pattern as a day rate.
2. Having no clear niche, so you are a generalist nobody thinks of first for anything in particular.
3. Staying a doer inside the client instead of an advisor, and executing tasks rather than shaping decisions.
4. Neglecting reputation and pipeline until the current engagement ends, then scrambling, which is how good consultants end up feast and famine.

You do not have to be near consulting to start building its foundation, and the single most useful thing you can do costs nothing and pays off for years: start making your judgement visible now. Write about what you know, share how you think about the problems in your work, teach what you have learned, wherever you are on the path. The consultant's central asset is a reputation that precedes them into the room, and that reputation is not built the year you go independent. It is built slowly, over years, by being publicly useful long before you need the return. The analyst who has spent years quietly sharing what they know arrives at the consulting stage with an audience already waiting, while the one who kept their head down arrives as an unknown, however brilliant. So pick one thing you understand well, and put it out into the world this week. It is the earliest possible down payment on the last stage of the path.
